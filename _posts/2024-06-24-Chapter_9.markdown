# Grow the Program, Not the Coach's Workload

## Three Weeks Before the Regional

Three weeks before its first regional tournament, Iron Pegasus did not look like a championship team.

The seven children were mostly rookies. They were the youngest of our three teams, and some learned and communicated in ways that required more patience and structure from the adults around them. Their Robot Game progress was slow. The operators were not yet consistent. Their project skit was chaotic. They had little idea how to turn months of work into Robot Design and Innovation Project reports.

Fire Kirin and Golden Phoenix were farther ahead. Iron Pegasus could see that every week because all three teams met in the same Hope Chinese School classroom.

That comparison could have discouraged them. It could also teach them.

I spent more time with Iron Pegasus during those final weeks than with either of the other teams. We rehearsed. I gave constructive feedback. They revised, tried again, and heard more feedback. Then we repeated the cycle.

The older teams showed them what a complete judging presentation looked like. Veteran students demonstrated how a two-and-a-half-minute Robot Game run should flow. Rookies no longer had to imagine what “ready” meant. They could watch it, question it, and attempt it themselves.

At the regional tournament, Iron Pegasus rose from the bottom of our three teams to receive the Champion's Award and first place in Robot Performance.

People sometimes call a turnaround like that a miracle.

Coaches should be suspicious of miracles.

A miracle makes the outcome sound sudden. The children experienced something much less magical and much more useful: a working learning system. They had a nearby example, repeated practice, specific feedback, a deadline, and a belief that being behind did not mean they belonged at the bottom.

Later that season, Iron Pegasus advanced to the 2026 FIRST Championship in Houston, where the team received the Rising All-Star Award and Coach Min Lu received the Coach/Mentor Award.

Those awards are part of the story. They are not the point of it.

The point is that Iron Pegasus did not grow in isolation. By the time we created our third team, we had also created something larger than any one roster: a program that could transfer knowledge from one generation of children to the next.

## The Ceiling of One Team

For years, Fire Kirin was the Hope Chinese School FLL program.

The class sometimes attracted more interested students than could compete together on one FIRST LEGO League Challenge team. Under current FIRST guidance, a traditional Challenge team has two to ten youth members; coaches should always verify the [current participation requirements](https://www.firstinspires.org/programs/fll/get-started) for their region and season.

The arithmetic created a coaching decision. We could turn children away, keep some students in a class without giving them the full competition experience, or build another team.

The decision became more urgent after Fire Kirin won the Arizona championship during the 2022-2023 *SUPERPOWERED* season. Success brought attention. Enrollment increased. More parents wanted their children in the class, and more children wanted the experience they had seen Fire Kirin earn.

It is tempting to interpret that demand as proof that a program is ready to expand.

Demand is only proof that people are interested.

The reason we felt ready was not only that the roster was full. Coach Min Lu and I had accumulated years of experience. We had team history, old robots, field models, presentations, reports, teaching materials, competition routines, and mistakes we could now explain. We believed we could support two teams without turning the second into an afterthought.

Golden Phoenix was created for the 2023-2024 *MASTERPIECE* season.

Every child on its first roster was a rookie.

Fire Kirin kept students who had competed the previous season or participated in my introductory FLL class during the spring. Other new students joined Golden Phoenix with a simple intention: they would learn FLL through a real season and a real competition, not by waiting until adults considered them experienced enough.

This was not a junior varsity team. It was a rookie team with full ownership of its own season.

That distinction matters.

If the new team exists only to support the veteran team, children recognize the hierarchy immediately. The older team receives the best table time, the newest parts, the coach's attention, and the ambitious expectations. The rookie team receives leftovers and advice.

We did not always distribute time perfectly, but we wanted both teams to encounter the complete FLL experience: Robot Game, Robot Design, Innovation Project, Core Values, judging, outreach, setbacks, and decisions that belonged to the children.

Golden Phoenix became the Arizona champion in its rookie season and later received the Engineering Excellence Award at the 2024 FIRST Championship.

The lesson was not that every rookie team will reach Houston.

The lesson was that “rookie” describes experience, not potential.

## Do Not Copy and Paste a Team

By the 2025-2026 *UNEARTHED* season, the program needed a third team.

The original plan resembled our Golden Phoenix model. Golden Phoenix would contain mostly veteran members. Iron Pegasus would become the rookie team. This time, however, I intentionally placed one veteran with Iron Pegasus so the youngest children would have a peer who understood the rhythm of a season.

The rosters changed again as the season developed. Golden Phoenix began with seven members. Later, after parent requests and other practical considerations, four remained with Golden Phoenix and three moved to Fire Kirin. Fire Kirin's new roster consisted of older rookies. Golden Phoenix had two veterans and two rookies. Iron Pegasus remained the youngest group and included children with varied learning and communication needs.

The reshuffling reminds us that a roster is not a spreadsheet exercise.

Age matters, but it is not the only factor. Experience matters, but a team of only veterans may inherit old habits along with old knowledge. Friendships matter, but allowing friendship to make every decision can create cliques. Parent logistics matter because children cannot attend meetings adults cannot support. Leadership matters, but placing all confident speakers and builders together can leave another team without a starting point.

A coach forming multiple teams is designing learning environments.

There is no formula that makes the decision painless. Explain the criteria before announcing the rosters. Listen to families, but do not promise that every request can define the team. Watch children work together before making permanent assignments. When possible, give them meaningful input.

Our approach has evolved into something that combines theater, code, and student choice.

In class, we borrow the Sorting Hat scene from *Harry Potter*. A Python program announces which team each student will initially join. The script includes an element of randomness, although the coaches also use what we know about the children. The ceremony makes the announcement playful and gives every team a story from its first moment.

After students have worked together, we may let them choose teammates through a process inspired by alliance selection in FIRST Robotics Competition. Children are not merely assigned forever by adults; they gain evidence about who communicates, follows through, shares credit, and helps under pressure.

The method is less important than its message:

**A team is something children help build, not a container adults put them in.**

## A Name Children Must Grow Into

Each of our teams has its own visual identity.

A girl on the original Fire Kirin team who loved drawing created its logo. Siblings helped design the Golden Phoenix mascot. Iron Pegasus used an AI image tool as part of developing its logo.

The method changed with the children and the time. The ownership did not.

A new team should not feel like “Fire Kirin Number Two.” Its members need a name they can call across a noisy gym, a shirt they helped imagine, and stories that belong to their roster. Identity creates emotional ownership before the team has trophies or technical achievements.

History then changes what the name means.

When new children join Fire Kirin, Golden Phoenix, or Iron Pegasus, they know earlier members carried that name to major events. The history can inspire them. It can also become a burden if adults use it carelessly.

“This team went to Houston, so you must win” is pressure.

“This team learned to recover, support one another, and explain its work; now you will add your chapter” is inheritance.

During the *UNEARTHED* season, every team included a girl. Each became an essential contributor or leader. That fact matters because families still sometimes arrive believing robotics is mainly for boys who already know how to code. Our own teams repeatedly contradict that assumption. Leadership appeared in technical work, organization, communication, project thinking, and the ability to hold a group together.

Do not recruit children to fit a robotics stereotype. Build a team broad enough to disprove it.

## Competitors Under One Roof

Three teams create more opportunities for unhealthy comparison.

Children notice which robot scores higher. Parents notice which team receives more coach time. Everyone notices awards. Shared kits, practice tables, meeting rooms, experts, and attention can make teammates feel like competitors before a tournament begins.

We return to the Core Values.

Fire Kirin, Golden Phoenix, and Iron Pegasus wear different shirts, but they sit in one classroom and learn from the same coaching group. They attend expert sessions and outreach activities together. They conduct friendly competitions and mock judging sessions. They exchange ideas. We want them to feel like branches of one family rather than three programs protecting secrets from one another.

That culture becomes visible in small engineering moments.

Early in the *UNEARTHED* season, Golden Phoenix learned useful attachment-design techniques from Iron Pegasus. Golden Phoenix later credited the other team while speaking with judges. That public credit mattered as much as the design lesson. It told the younger children, “Your idea was valuable,” and told the judges, “We know collaboration does not erase ownership.”

Golden Phoenix and Fire Kirin met archaeology experts together. They discussed their Innovation Projects in the classroom and raised questions that improved one another's thinking. Robot Game operators switched practice tables so programs and attachments had to work outside their most familiar environment. Teams compared their Challenge sets to check whether models had been built and positioned consistently.

At one regional tournament, Fire Kirin discovered that a mission model on its practice field had been assembled incorrectly. The mistake had shaped the team's testing and cost points at competition. Before the state championship, Golden Phoenix and Iron Pegasus operators helped Fire Kirin correct the model and verify the full field setup.

They were helping a team they might be compared against later.

That is not charity. It is Gracious Professionalism expressed through calibration, LEGO pieces, and shared attention.

Multiple teams can create an advantage no single team can reproduce easily:

- More than one robot and attachment architecture to study.
- More practice tables on which to expose fragile assumptions.
- Built-in audiences for project feedback and judging rehearsals.
- Veteran students who can translate a coach's lesson into a child's language.
- Enough people to make scrimmages feel real.
- A larger network of parents, experts, meeting spaces, and skills.

The advantage exists only if the teams share.

## A Veteran Is a Bridge, Not a Substitute Coach

Placing a veteran on a rookie team can accelerate learning. It can also create a child who makes every decision because “we did it this way last year.”

Veterans need an assigned teaching role and the same boundaries we give adult mentors.

I ask experienced students to teach short technical lessons to the combined class. Shiyu from Golden Phoenix presented acceleration and deceleration programming together with PID-controlled movement. The topic was new and technically difficult that season, so teaching it required him to organize his own understanding. An Iron Pegasus veteran taught foundational CAD and base-robot design. Veteran operators also demonstrated how to organize a complete two-and-a-half-minute run.

Teaching is evidence of learning, but the veteran should not become the author of the rookie team's work.

A useful student mentor can:

- Demonstrate a concept, then ask the rookies to build their own version.
- Explain a failure from a previous season without insisting on the old solution.
- Ask what the team tested before offering an answer.
- Observe a practice run and give one specific piece of feedback.
- Show how to use a tool, rulebook, or report without completing the task.

A student mentor has crossed the line when the rookies wait for that child to design, code, speak, or decide for them.

The goal is not to make the rookie team dependent on a younger expert. It is to shorten the distance between “I have never seen this” and “I can try this myself.”

## The Adults Must Become a Team, Too

A three-team program cannot be sustained by one adult moving faster.

Hope Chinese School provides a classroom where all teams meet weekly. Parents provide additional space in their homes for team meetings. Coach Min and I travel among those meetings, check progress, and adjust our attention according to what each team needs.

At home, Min and I discuss assignments, schedules, and the previous week's progress. I lead much of the technical teaching. Min leads team scheduling, competition-day planning, and much of the project and material management. She pays close attention to details, follows unfinished assignments, and has the authority to make children—and adults—take a deadline seriously. I take most of the photographs. A parent serves as finance officer. Other parents provide space, transportation, expertise, and emotional support.

Min's competition schedules are detailed enough that a parent can look at the table and know where the team must be and what happens next. That document is more than logistics. It protects the children's attention. They do not have to absorb adult confusion while preparing for a match or judging session.

Our strengths are complementary. Technical knowledge alone does not run a program. Neither does a perfect calendar. Children need both inspiration and follow-through, flexibility and consistency, ambition and the adult who remembers that the prototype must actually reach the venue.

Parent mentors often begin as adults willing to spend extra time with the children. Willingness is necessary, but it is not training.

Every mentor must understand the six Core Values, Gracious Professionalism, and the boundary that all judged work belongs to the children. We hold parent meetings and point new adults to the free introductory FLL seminars I teach each summer and publish online. A future version of this book may become another part of that orientation.

Before adding teams, develop adults who can answer three questions consistently:

1. What may I teach?
2. What must the children decide and do?
3. How will I respond when their process is slower than mine?

If adults give different answers, adding another roster multiplies the confusion.

## Scale the Learning System

The wrong way to grow a program is to copy every demand placed on the founding coach.

One team asks the coach to review code, so three teams send every program to the same adult. One team needs a judging rehearsal, so the coach becomes the only audience for three presentations. One parent asks a scheduling question, so every family messages the coach separately. Growth becomes a larger pile of work with one name at the center.

The better question is: **What allowed the first team to learn, and how can that become a system others can use?**

Our current answers include:

- A shared weekly class for concepts that benefit everyone.
- Separate team meetings for ownership and focused work.
- Veteran-led demonstrations with clear boundaries.
- Cross-team mock judging and scrimmages.
- Shared experts and outreach opportunities.
- Multiple practice tables and deliberate table swapping.
- Common expectations based on Core Values.
- Parent roles for finance, space, transportation, and logistics.
- Written schedules and assignments rather than memory alone.
- Reports, photographs, code, and season history that make past learning visible.

The chapter is still incomplete here because our system is still incomplete.

Some responsibilities remain too dependent on Coach Min or me. Equipment, data, communication, succession, and coach workload need the same honest engineering process we ask children to use: identify the failure point, test a better method, document it, and improve it next season.

That is not an embarrassing admission. It is the central challenge of building anything meant to last.

## Should You Start a Second Team?

Do not expand only because the first team won.

Do not expand only because ten more families asked.

Do not expand because managing multiple team numbers looks like proof of a successful program.

A first-time coach should consider a second team when several conditions are becoming true:

- Enough children need a genuine competition experience that one roster cannot serve them well.
- At least one additional trained adult can own real responsibilities without doing children's work.
- Both teams will have reliable meeting space, equipment access, and parent support.
- The program has a repeatable way to teach foundational skills.
- Coach time can be allocated without making one team permanently secondary.
- Each roster can develop its own identity and make meaningful decisions.
- Shared resources have owners, schedules, and rules.
- The adults can explain how rivalry will be handled through Core Values.

Wait another season if the founding team still depends on the coach for every decision, if parents do not understand the commitment, if meeting space is uncertain, or if the second team would receive only whatever time remains.

Scaling magnifies culture.

If children already share credit, document work, welcome rookies, and recover from mistakes, growth gives those habits a larger stage. If adults take over, information lives in one person's head, and winning determines status, growth multiplies those problems too.

## What Rose from the Bottom

Iron Pegasus did not succeed because our program had become perfect.

The team succeeded inside a system built from nearly a decade of experiments—some successful, some painful. Fire Kirin provided history. Golden Phoenix proved that rookies could own ambitious work. Veteran students made difficult ideas visible. Parents opened their homes. Hope Chinese School gave the teams a common classroom. Coach Min created order around the deadlines. The children did the repetitions.

Three weeks before the regional, Iron Pegasus could see how far it still had to go.

It could also see teams beside it showing that the distance was possible.

That may be the greatest advantage of growing from a team into a program. The coach is no longer the only person who can say, “I have seen children learn this.”

The children can say it to one another.

And then the newest team becomes part of the evidence.
