## The Child Is the Final Product

At the beginning of the season, Shiyu could barely introduce himself.

When it was his turn to speak, he was very shy. Even saying his name in front of the team felt difficult. If you had met him then, you probably would not have guessed that a few months later he would be explaining the team's acceleration and deceleration code, and how it worked together with PID control. You certainly would not have guessed that he would speak in a judging room at the FIRST LEGO League World Championship in Houston.

But that is exactly what happened.

Shiyu was a member of Golden Phoenix (#61064), a rookie team in the 2023–2024 *MASTERPIECE* season. During one practice, I asked him to step forward and teach the class how the team's acceleration and deceleration code worked together with PID control. Acceleration and deceleration control was new for the team that season and remained one of its technical difficulties. It was not a pretend assignment invented to make him feel included. It was real technical work that the team needed to understand. He prepared, stood in front of everyone, and gave a good demonstration.

Later, during a competition judging session, the judges asked the familiar final question: "What else would you like us to know?" Shiyu realized that the team had not said enough about Core Values. He reminded his teammates to talk about them. That small moment of awareness may have helped the judges understand the complete team—not only its robot and project, but also how its members worked and grew together.

At the World Championship that same rookie season, Shiyu told the judges that FLL had changed his personality. The child who once struggled to introduce himself had found his voice. In later seasons, he became a team leader.

That is why I coach.

The robot matters. The Innovation Project matters. Competition matters, too—no honest competitive coach should pretend otherwise. Awards can motivate a team, and we are proud when our children's hard work is recognized. But the robot is not the final product.

The child is the final product.

This is the philosophy at the center of this book: **we do not use kids to build robots; we use robots to build kids.**

## Welcome to the Wonderful Chaos

Welcome to the world of FIRST LEGO League (FLL)—where robots roll, ideas soar, and coaches sometimes wonder whether caffeine counts as a food group.

If you are reading this book, there is a good chance that someone has asked you to coach a team. Maybe you volunteered enthusiastically. Maybe you are a teacher or engineer who wants to give something back. Maybe you are a parent who was "voluntold" because your child likes LEGO bricks. You may be excited, confused, or already staring at a box of parts and wondering what you have gotten yourself into.

I know the feeling.

You may believe that an FLL coach must be the best programmer in the room. You may worry that you cannot answer every engineering question. You may think a successful season begins with a perfect robot plan.

It does not.

A coach does not need to hold every answer. A coach needs to create the conditions in which children learn how to find answers together. We show them the rope. We demonstrate important concepts, spark their interest, and help them believe that the problem is worth solving. Then we let them climb. We stay close enough to give frequent and constructive feedback, but we do not climb for them.

That boundary sounds simple. In practice, it may be the hardest part of coaching.

When a robot fails one week before competition, an adult can often repair it faster. When a presentation is disorganized, an adult can rewrite it in an evening. When a prototype looks unfinished beside the polished work of another team, every instinct tells us to step in.

But if we take ownership away from the children, we may improve the object while weakening the child. A shinier prototype is not worth that trade.

This book is about how to guide without taking over, compete without losing perspective, and build a team in which every child has meaningful work and a voice.

## I Entered FLL Through the Judging Room

My FLL journey began in 2017, when my former colleague and friend Tom Situ introduced me to the program. I volunteered as a judge at the Arizona state championship at Arizona State University.

I arrived as a curious volunteer. By the end of the day, I was hooked.

The students' energy was impossible to miss. They had designed robots, researched real problems, tested ideas, survived disagreements, and somehow organized all of that learning into presentations for a room full of adults. Some robots performed beautifully. Others developed what I will politely call "competition-day personalities." Yet the most impressive part was not any single machine.

It was the children.

I saw students thinking under pressure, building on one another's answers, and recovering when something did not go as planned. I saw the values FIRST calls Discovery, Innovation, Impact, Inclusion, Teamwork, and Fun. I saw Gracious Professionalism—the belief that we can compete intensely while respecting and helping one another.

Judging also taught me something I later had to relearn as a coach: judges are not looking only for a final answer. They are trying to understand the team's journey. Who did the work? How did the design change? What did the team learn from failure? Can every child explain a meaningful contribution? Do the Core Values appear only on a poster, or can they be seen in the way the team behaves?

The engineering process matters more than a perfect-looking result. Core Values are visible everywhere—inside and outside the judging room, at the practice table, in the pit, and at the Robot Game table.

By the end of that state championship, I did not want to remain only an observer. I wanted to coach.

## Fire Kirin: Where We Learned by Making Mistakes

I approached the principal of Hope Chinese School in Arizona, and together we formed Fire Kirin (#33274) in 2017. My daughter joined the team and remained part of its journey through the 2022–2023 *SUPERPOWERED* season.

Like many rookie coaches, I began with enthusiasm and incomplete understanding. Our first season moved slowly. We struggled with time management and student commitment. Not enough children were involved deeply enough, early enough. At a regional competition, one team member told the judges that the coach had designed the robot. That was not true, but the statement revealed a real failure in our process: the student did not understand the team's work well enough to explain it.

We did not advance to the state championship.

It was painful, but it became one of my most important coaching lessons. If only a few students understand how the robot was created, then the team does not fully own the robot—even when the coach never touched the design. Children need meaningful responsibilities throughout the season. They need to document their decisions, learn one another's work, and practice speaking before judging day.

We learned to plan earlier. We learned to involve more students. We learned that judging preparation is not about memorizing a polished script; it is about helping children recognize and communicate what they genuinely did and learned.

Fire Kirin grew with us. Over the years, the team earned awards for its robot work, creativity, and resilience. In the 2022–2023 *SUPERPOWERED* season, Fire Kirin won the Arizona Champion's Award and advanced to the World Championship in Houston. My daughter finished her Fire Kirin journey on that world stage—six seasons after our difficult beginning.

## Golden Phoenix: Excellence Without Taking Over

In the 2023–2024 *MASTERPIECE* season, we created Golden Phoenix (#61064). This time, we were not beginning from zero. We brought lessons from Fire Kirin: assign real responsibilities, involve every student, document the process, prepare everyone to speak, and pay attention to the whole FLL program—not only the Robot Game leaderboard.

![Golden Phoenix team mascot]({{ site.url }}{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/gp-mascot.png){: width="500" height="300"}

Golden Phoenix won the Arizona Champion's Award and advanced to the World Championship in its rookie season. But before Houston, the team had a serious weakness: its Innovation Project prototype was not where we wanted it to be.

The mentors demonstrated how SPIKE Prime parts could be used for prototyping. We showed examples from past projects to inspire ideas. The children made progress, but they did not arrive with the kind of shiny, fully developed prototype we saw from some other world-level teams.

It would have been easy for adults to finish it.

We did not.

The children presented the work they actually understood and owned. Golden Phoenix earned the Engineering Excellence Award at the World Championship—an award recognizing strong achievement across the program. Their success did not mean the prototype was secretly perfect. It meant that excellence is larger than one artifact. The judges saw engineering, learning, teamwork, communication, and potential in a complete rookie team.

That season also gave us Shiyu's transformation. To me, his new confidence was more important than any trophy.

## Iron Pegasus: A Rookie Team Built on Years of Lessons

Iron Pegasus (#72674) began in the 2025–2026 *UNEARTHED* season. It was a new team, but our coaching philosophy was no longer new. Iron Pegasus inherited lessons earned through years of mistakes, reflection, and growth.

The team advanced to the World Championship in Houston and received the Rising All-Star Award. My wife, Coach Min Lu, received the Coach/Mentor Award there.

## What I Believe About Coaching

Nearly a decade of coaching and judging has not given me a perfect formula. Every team is different, every season brings new surprises, and children are very talented at discovering problems no coaching manual anticipated. But six principles guide my work.

### 1. Show Them the Rope, but Let Them Climb

Introduce the concept. Demonstrate a technique. Ask questions that open a path. Give frequent, specific, and constructive feedback. Then return ownership to the children.

"Kids do the work" does not mean adults do nothing. It means adults teach without secretly becoming team members.

### 2. Make Ownership Visible

Our teams use a RACI chart so students, mentors, and coaches understand their responsibilities and boundaries. For each major job, we identify who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed.

Every student holds several roles. One may program missions and help document the engineering process. Another may lead research, contact professionals, and prepare outreach materials. Roles do not create status. They create ownership.

When ownership is clear, children can honestly say, "This is what I did, this is what I learned, and this is how my work helped the team."

### 3. Talent Never Excuses Poor Values

The six FLL Core Values and Gracious Professionalism are not decorations for a presentation board. They are our behavioral foundation. A talented programmer does not receive permission to disrespect a teammate. A high Robot Game score does not excuse mocking another team.

When behavior violates our values, we address it directly. The goal is accountability, reflection, and repairing the team—not humiliation. Core Values matter most in the moments when following them is difficult.

### 4. Give Every Child a Voice

Dominant students naturally fill silence. Quiet students can disappear inside it. A coach must design opportunities for everyone to contribute.

We assign homework, presentations, and small-group projects so that each student has a chance to produce and explain meaningful work. This is good preparation for judging, where every child should contribute, but the purpose is larger. A child cannot become a leader if the team never makes room for that child's voice.

### 5. Define Winning as Beating Yesterday

Winning is a healthy motivator, but our definition must be broad enough to help children grow. Did the robot become more reliable? Did the team recover from conflict more constructively? Did a quiet student speak? Did the children ask better questions than they asked last month?

Compete against other teams on tournament day. Compete against yesterday every day.

Awards are consequences of hard work—and sometimes luck. They are not proof of a child's value. There is no meaningless competition if the team learns from it.

### 6. Reflect, Especially When It Hurts

Saying "everyone is a winner" should never become an excuse to avoid honest evaluation. After a competition or season, we ask what went well, what did not, and what we will change next time.

Our first Fire Kirin season taught us about time management, commitment, shared ownership, and judging preparation. Those lessons helped later teams reach places we could not imagine in 2017. Reflection turns a disappointing result into future capability.

The real journey is not from regional competition to state championship to Houston. It is from one version of the team to a better one.

## A Book for the First-Time Volunteer Coach

This book is for the coach standing at the beginning of that journey.

We will discuss how to recruit a team, work with parents, divide responsibilities, plan a season, guide robot design and programming, approach the Innovation Project, teach Core Values, prepare for judging, and survive competition day with both your team spirit and your sense of humor intact.

I will write from two sides of the judging-room door. As a coach, I know the pressure of helping children prepare while respecting the boundary that the work must remain theirs. As a judge, I know that a polished presentation cannot replace authentic understanding—and that a nervous child may know much more than the first answer reveals.

I will also share our mistakes. A book containing only championship stories would teach the wrong lesson. Fire Kirin, Golden Phoenix, and Iron Pegasus reached the World Championship, but none of them traveled a straight road. Their difficult moments are often more useful than their trophies.

You do not need to be an expert in everything before you begin. You need patience, curiosity, clear boundaries, and the courage to let children struggle long enough to surprise you.

There will be robot meltdowns, unfinished presentations, disagreements, missing LEGO pieces, and a few extra gray hairs. There will also be moments like Shiyu standing in Houston and telling the judges that FLL changed him.

Those moments make the chaos worthwhile.
